# One central problem per experience

Use this during discovery and story synthesis. The user's narrative preference is one central problem for each experience, not one technical cause or one action. A large project may contain several such stories.

## Find the user-level anchor

Start with who was trying to do what, in which situation, and where they were prevented from completing it. Prefer a concrete blocked user goal to “improve UX,” “increase engagement,” “modernize technology,” or the absence of a proposed feature. Internal operators and other real product users count as users. Do not invent an end-user pain point when only an operational or business problem is supported; identify the actual affected actor.

When several issues are listed, search for the nearest meaningful common user problem, not the most abstract possible purpose. A root user problem is not necessarily the deepest technical root cause. One user problem can have multiple contributing causes and multiple solutions.

## Recover relationships before polishing

Distinguish:

- Central problem: the user's blocked goal or consequential difficulty.
- Symptom: what was observed, such as abandonment or repeated requests.
- Cause or mechanism: what plausibly produced that difficulty.
- Constraint: what limited the solution, such as latency, capacity, or implementation time.
- Subsequent obstacle: what appeared or became relevant after an attempted solution.
- Independent problem: an issue with a different user goal and no established causal connection.

Keep occurrence time, discovery time, and intervention time separate. An issue discovered later may have existed earlier. A problem handled second was not necessarily caused by the first solution. Several causes can coexist; do not manufacture a serial chain to simplify the story.

Ask the next question from the biggest missing relationship, one at a time:

- “기술 이야기를 빼면, 사용자는 무엇을 하려다가 어디서 막혔어요?”
- “이 두 현상 때문에 사용자가 못 하게 된 일은 같은 건가요?”
- “그 문제는 원래 있었나요, 첫 해결책을 적용한 뒤에 생겼나요?”
- “왜 그걸 먼저 해결했고, 해결한 뒤에는 무엇이 남았어요?”
- “이 제약을 풀면 처음 사용자 문제가 어떻게 줄어드는 거죠?”

Propose a candidate central problem and a provisional relationship map early; revise it with the user. Do not settle for the first plausible umbrella statement. Do not force a repeated why-question ritual or demand a fully polished answer from the user.

## Construct a persuasive sequence

Present user situation and observed difficulty → diagnosis and cause branches → priority decision → attempted solution → remaining or newly introduced obstacle → adjustment → result against the original problem. Use only the stages that occurred. A concise branching outline can make the relationship clearer than a flat list.

Label inferred mechanisms as hypotheses until supported. Preserve the difference between a retrospective narrative structure and the actual decision chronology. If issues share only a company, product, slogan, or broad KPI, split or select stories instead of pretending they form one problem. A selected sub-story still needs its own single, concrete central problem.

## Test whether a stranger would believe it

Check whether an unfamiliar listener could answer:

1. Who was affected, what were they trying to do, and why did the failure matter?
2. What observation supports the problem rather than merely the chosen solution?
3. Why would the diagnosed cause produce that user difficulty?
4. Why does each action address that cause, and why was it taken then?
5. Does the outcome concern the same user problem, population, and measurement scope?

Probe the weakest bridge with a specific skeptical question. Do not present a checklist in every mentoring turn. Strong wording cannot repair a missing causal link; offer a narrower defensible formulation or investigate the missing detail.
